### Reading the job and scoring it (Nelly Kerubo's post is a model for the scoring mode)

- **"The job title is not the job."** Break the job ad down into the actual mix of work (for example: programme design + government engagement + resource mobilisation + climate/DRR).
- **Tailor to the hiring problem, not the job title.** Ask what problem the organisation is hiring someone to solve.
- **Check these five before spending hours tailoring** (these become hard filters in the scoring mode):
  1. contract category (for example NO-D = national officer)
  2. nationality eligibility
  3. language requirements
  4. technical specialisation
  5. who you'd actually be working with (ministries, JICA, local government, private sector)
- Kamran's Step 1 also asks what the **organisation's mandate and donors** say about the profile they want. Job scoring should always do this.

### Cover letter

- One page. Open with **why this organisation's mandate matters to you, and why this role now**. Then three short paragraphs, each linking one key requirement to a specific example. Don't repeat the CV, avoid clichés, and **sound like a practitioner, not a template** (Kamran Shah).
- A warm, human tone with no corporate clichés (Cyberman). In our sector, "corporate" tone is itself a problem.

### Interview

- **"Why should we hire you?"** Don't recite your CV. Show you've researched their mission and current work, then link a concrete result of yours to that mission (Deepali Vyas via How To Find A Job). This fits the sector naturally.
- Opening phrases that help structure answers: "My role in this project was…", "The result was…", "I haven't done that directly, but here's how I'd approach it…" (hCalls). Useful for STAR coaching.
- Answers for career gaps and redundancy (Nina Devouge): state the dates, give a short honest reason, and say what you did during the gap. **Very relevant to clients hit by funding cuts.** Phrases like "my role was made redundant as part of a restructure" become a script in the system.
- Final step before sending anything: a **stress test**. Review the documents as a hiring manager shortlisting 200 applications, then fix what would get them rejected (Kamran Shah).

### AI workflow (the prompt sequences you saved)

Three of your saved posts give AI prompt sequences. **Kamran Shah's is the one written for our sector.** The six steps match career-ops' pipeline and can become the core of the system:

1. **Decode the job** (acting as a senior recruiter in humanitarian and development work): top 10 requirements, competencies, keywords, what the mandate and donors say about the profile wanted.
2. **Honest gap analysis**: a table of requirement / evidence / strong-partial-missing.
3. **AI interviews the client** to fill gaps: budget, team, donors, reach, partners, locations, results.
4. **Rewrite the CV**: 2 pages, 4-line profile, action + scale + result, [CONFIRM] for anything uncertain.
5. **Cover letter**: mandate + why now, 3 requirement→example paragraphs.
6. **Stress test** as the shortlisting manager, then fix.

Your own extras on top of this would be: your positioning method, the four client segments, the CV formats by employer type, and your list of words to avoid. That's where you beat the free prompt posts. Your website already makes this point: AI CVs all sound the same.

### What to avoid (from the generic posts you saved)

- Lists of remote tech jobs and "33 sites" posts (Minal Thakur, Karina Rakulenko, Altiam Kabir). They're generic, full of links, and don't fit the sector.
- Ending a post with a giveaway or tool promotion (Cyberman, Thomas Pearce). It looks cheap next to sector credibility.
- Phone numbers and WhatsApp sales pitches at the end of advice posts. Use one clean CTA instead: free 20-minute call.
