## 1. Where tests sit in NGO hiring

Most NGOs follow the same five steps as the rest of the sector: screening, a test, a competency interview, checks, then an offer (research/03 §1 point 1, §2). What the research found at three large INGOs:

| Employer | Tests and interviews | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **NRC** (Norwegian Refugee Council) | Tests chosen to fit the role: aptitude (numerical, verbal, abstract), personality, technical tests (for example education, security, finance) and language tests, often during the interview. Usually two interviews, with HR, a technical specialist and the hiring manager. Field candidates are also asked about stress, resilience and health. The whole process can take up to four months. | research/03 §6.1 |
| **NORCAP** (NRC's expert roster) | A video interview, a personality assessment, technical tests, then a **job simulation** and a structured competency interview. | research/03 §6.1 |
| **IRC** (International Rescue Committee) | Usually three rounds: the recruiter, the hiring manager, then a deeper role-specific session. Later rounds can be panels. | research/03 §6.2 |
| **Save the Children** | Most processes in Australia have 2 interviews, sometimes a 3rd or psychometric testing. Field offices often add a written test (secondary, crowd-sourced source). | research/03 §6.3 |

Many humanitarian employers share one competency framework, the **Core Humanitarian Competency Framework**. It exists in English, Arabic, French and other languages (research/03 §6.4). It is worth reading before any humanitarian test or interview.

## 2. What written tasks look like (read this warning first)

In practice, NGO field tests often ask you to draft a short concept note, a logframe, an M&E plan or a budget narrative, or to analyse a scenario, within 1-3 hours. **This is general sector knowledge, not something the research confirmed from an employer's own page** (research/03 §6.5). Formats vary by employer, role and country.

So: **the invitation is the only reliable guide.** Read it with Kristin. Share it with her (with your consent) so she can match your practice to it.

Two kinds of task:
- **Timed test.** You get the task at a set time and submit within a fixed window, often online.
- **Take-home task.** You get a brief and a deadline, sometimes a day or more. The deadline and the page limit are in the invitation.

## 3. The task types, one by one

The structures below are standard practice in the sector. Use them unless the brief gives its own template. If it does, follow the brief.

### 3.1 Concept note or short proposal

**What it is:** a 1-3 page pitch for a project to a donor.

**A common structure.**
1. Problem: who is affected, where, and how badly. Use the numbers you are given.
2. Objective: what will change, for whom, by when.
3. Approach: main activities and why they will work.
4. Partners and target group: who, how many, how chosen.
5. Results: 2-4 measurable results.
6. Budget estimate and duration, if asked.
7. Why your organisation: one short paragraph.

**Common mistakes:** a long problem section and a thin approach; activities that do not lead to the objective; no numbers; ignoring the donor's priorities named in the brief.

### 3.2 Report writing (progress report, situation report, summary)

**What it is:** turning raw information into a clear document for a named reader.

**A common structure.** Summary first (3-5 lines). Then what happened against plan, what changed, problems and how they were handled, and next steps. Tables for numbers.

**Common mistakes:** listing activities without results; hiding problems; writing for yourself, not the reader.

### 3.3 Case study or scenario analysis

**What it is:** a situation (a security incident, a partner problem, a funding cut, a community complaint) and the question "what do you do?"

**A common structure.**
1. Restate the problem in two lines.
2. Name what you know, what you do not know, and who is affected.
3. Give 2-3 options, with risks.
4. Recommend one and say why.
5. List the first actions, in order, with who does what.
6. Say who you would inform and when.

**Common mistakes:** jumping to a solution; forgetting safety, safeguarding or the people the project serves; no decision at the end.

### 3.4 Logframe

**What it is:** a table that links what a project does to what it changes.

| Level | Description | Indicators | Means of verification | Assumptions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Goal (impact) | The long-term change the project contributes to | | | |
| Outcome | The change the project itself achieves | | | |
| Outputs | What the project delivers | | | |
| Activities | What the team does | | | |

**Rules of thumb.**
- Each level must lead logically to the one above it.
- Indicators are specific and measurable, with a baseline and a target where the brief gives you data.
- Means of verification are real sources: attendance lists, surveys, reports.
- Assumptions are conditions outside the project's control.

**Common mistakes:** outputs written as activities ("training held" instead of "200 teachers trained"); indicators with no number; assumptions that are really risks the project should manage.

### 3.5 M&E plan

**What it is:** how the project will track its indicators. For each indicator: definition, baseline, target, data source, method, frequency, who collects it, and how results are used.

**Common mistake:** a plan that collects data nobody uses.

### 3.6 Budget or budget narrative

**What it is:** a budget table built from activities, or a text that explains a budget you are given.

**Rules of thumb.**
- Every cost links to an activity in the plan.
- Show units: quantity x unit cost x duration.
- Separate staff, activity, equipment, travel and support costs if the brief does not give categories.
- The totals must add up. Check them twice.
- The narrative explains **why** each main line is needed and how the cost was estimated.

**Common mistakes:** totals that do not match; costs with no activity; a narrative that repeats the table instead of explaining it.

## 4. How to prepare in 48 hours

Use this when an invitation gives you two days before a timed test, or when a take-home task has a deadline about 48 hours away. Always check the real deadline in the invitation first.

### If the test is in 48 hours

| When | What you do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Hour 0-1 | Read the invitation twice. Write down: format, time limit, language, platform, whether AI or notes are allowed, file format. Ask the employer if any of it is unclear. | 1 h |
| Hour 1-2 | Send the invitation to Kristin and book the pre-test call (every programme includes this call). | 15 min |
| Day 1 | Research the employer: its strategy, its work in the country, its main donors and the job description. Write one page of notes. | 2 h |
| Day 1 | One timed practice task of the type you expect, under the real time limit. | 1-3 h |
| Day 1 evening | Self-mark against the checklist in section 5. Send it to Kristin if there is time. | 30 min |
| Day 2 | Pre-test call with Kristin. Fix the one weakest point from the practice. | 20 min |
| Day 2 | Tech check: computer, internet, power backup, quiet room, the platform log-in. | 30 min |
| Day 2 evening | Stop. Sleep. No more practice. | |

### If you have a 48-hour take-home task

| When | What you do | Share of time |
|---|---|---|
| First 2 hours | Read the brief. List every question it asks and every limit (pages, words, format). Write the outline. Send questions to the employer early if something is unclear. | 10% |
| Next block | Draft every section, roughly, before polishing any of them. | 50% |
| Sleep | Sleep on the draft. | |
| Next block | Rewrite. Check numbers, logic and the reader's needs. | 25% |
| Last block | Run the checklist in section 5. Format, name the file as asked, submit early. | 15% |

**Do not work the full 48 hours.** A rested, clear document beats an exhausted, long one.

## 5. Quality checklist (run it before you submit)

**The five marking points** Kristin uses for every practice task. They come from the EU essay "anchors", used here as a rubric (research/03 §4.1, §9 module B):

- [ ] **Logical structure.** Headings follow the brief's questions in order.
- [ ] **Concision.** No sentence repeats another. Within the page or word limit.
- [ ] **Clarity.** Short sentences. Terms explained once.
- [ ] **Fit to audience and purpose.** Written for the reader named in the brief.
- [ ] **Good use of the material provided.** You used the data and documents you were given, and did not ignore any.

**Task checks.**
- [ ] Every question in the brief has an answer.
- [ ] Every number is correct, and totals add up.
- [ ] Logframe levels link logically. Indicators have numbers.
- [ ] Budget lines link to activities.
- [ ] Safety, safeguarding and the people served are considered where relevant.
- [ ] Spelling and grammar checked in the language of the task.
- [ ] File format, file name and length as the brief asks.

**Rules checks.**
- [ ] **No AI unless the invitation explicitly allows it.** AI is banned in any test where it is not explicitly allowed (research/03 §3.7).
- [ ] **No confidential material.** Do not paste text, data or templates from your current or past employer's internal documents.
- [ ] **All your own work.** Nothing copied from public sources without saying so.

## 7. After the test

- Note down the task, the time and how it went, the same day. With your consent, Kristin adds the format (not the employer's confidential content) to her prep bank.
- Prepare for checks. Many INGOs use the Misconduct Disclosure Scheme, which needs your consent before reference checks start (research/03 §6.4). Save the Children Australia lists a police check, the scheme, 2 professional referees and signing the Child Safeguarding Policy (research/03 §6.3). Have referees and a police certificate ready (research/03 §1 point 6).
- Expect silence. NRC answers within about a month after screening, and no answer means you were not selected. Only interviewed candidates get feedback (research/03 §6.1). Silence is normal, not a verdict on you.
