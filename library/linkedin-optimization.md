**Three rules for everything in this guide**

1. **Tell the truth.** Your profile, your CV and what you say in interviews must match. Employers check references, and embassies run security vetting (research/03 §2, §5.5). A gap between your LinkedIn and your CV raises questions you do not need.
2. **Use AI to polish, not to invent.** UNDP's rules allow AI for proofreading and structure. They do not allow inventing or inflating experience (research/03 §3.7). Treat LinkedIn the same way.
3. **You stay in control.** You make every change and publish every post yourself. Kristin does not post for you.

### Checklist 1: Headline

Your headline appears under your name everywhere: in search results, in comments, in connection requests. It is the most-read line on your profile.

- [ ] Says the role you are going **for**, not only the role you have now.
- [ ] Uses the words from your target adverts (for example "partnerships", "MEAL", "grants", "CSR").
- [ ] Names 1-2 strengths with proof (a field, a donor type, a result).
- [ ] If your field was among the hardest hit (project lead and M&E roles), add a bridge to areas still hiring, such as partnerships, fundraising, data or short-term work (research/01 §8.1 point 4, §7).
- [ ] Lists your working languages if they matter for your targets. For MENA roles, Arabic is a strength to name (research/01 §8.1 point 8).
- [ ] Does **not** say "looking for opportunities", "aspiring" or "unemployed". The Open to Work setting does that job.
- [ ] Fits in the space LinkedIn gives you. Check the counter when you edit. Limits change.

### Checklist 2: About section

Your About section is a short story in your own voice. Use "I". Keep paragraphs short.

- [ ] **First 2 lines:** who you are and what you are going for. LinkedIn hides the rest behind "see more", so these lines must work alone.
- [ ] **Middle:** 3 proofs. One result with a number, one skill, one thing that shows why you care.
- [ ] **What you are looking for:** role type, employer type, location or remote.
- [ ] **One line on how to reach you** (a professional email, if you choose to share one).
- [ ] Matches your positioning statement and your CV.
- [ ] No confidential details: no internal donor figures, no names of people you served, nothing your employer has not made public.
- [ ] Written in the language of your main target. If you target two languages, see [LINKEDIN-SECOND-LANGUAGE-RULE].

**Before and after (fictional, segment A)**

> **Before:** I am a motivated and hardworking graduate passionate about making a difference. I am a team player with excellent communication skills. I am looking for opportunities in the humanitarian sector.

> **After:** I help programme teams turn field data into clear reports. I am looking for my first role in MEAL or programme support with an NGO or UN agency in [CITY] or remote.
>
> During my internship at [NGO-NAME], I cleaned and analysed survey data from [NUMBER] households and drafted the monthly report that went to the donor. Before that, I volunteered with [ORGANISATION] on [TOPIC].
>
> I work in Arabic, English and French. I am completing [COURSE-NAME] to strengthen my data skills.
>
> I am happy to talk with anyone working in MEAL or humanitarian programmes in the region: [YOUR-EMAIL].

Why the "after" works: it names a target, it proves skills with facts, and it ends with an open door. Every fact in it must be true for you.

### Checklist 3: Experience entries

- [ ] Job titles and dates match your CV exactly.
- [ ] Each recent role has 3-5 bullet points. Older roles can have 1-2.
- [ ] Each bullet starts with an action verb and ends with a result.
- [ ] Results show efficiency or donor value where you can: time saved, money managed, people reached, reports delivered on time. Employers now want "doing more with less" (research/01 §8.1 points 1 and 3).
- [ ] Use the words from your target adverts. Screening checks applications against the advert's requirements first (research/03 §2).
- [ ] Internships, volunteering and consulting count. List them as experience.
- [ ] If your contract ended in the funding cuts, you can say so in one neutral line. Do not explain more.

### Checklist 4: Skills

- [ ] Pick skills that appear in your target adverts. Use the advert's exact words.
- [ ] Put your most relevant skills at the top.
- [ ] Link each skill to the job or course where you used it, when LinkedIn offers that option.
- [ ] Remove skills you would not want to be tested on in an interview.
- [ ] Add your languages in the Languages section, with honest levels.
- [ ] Add certificates in "Licenses and certifications". If your gap analysis gave you a course or certificate plan, add each one when you finish it.

### Checklist 5: Featured section

The Featured section sits near the top of your profile. Use it to show work, not to tell.

- [ ] 1-3 items, no more.
- [ ] Good choices: a public report or publication you wrote or co-wrote; a writing sample you are allowed to share; a certificate; a short post about your work; a portfolio link.
- [ ] Only share documents that are already public, or that your employer has cleared.
- [ ] No photos of the people you served, especially children, unless your organisation has their consent on file and allows the use.

### Checklist 6: Photo and banner

**Photo**
- [ ] Recent, with your face clearly visible. Head and shoulders.
- [ ] Plain or calm background. Natural light, facing a window, works well.
- [ ] Dress as you would for a meeting with your target employer.
- [ ] No sunglasses, no group photo cropped down, no filters that change your face.
- [ ] Photo visibility set so recruiters can see it (see the settings checklist).
- [ ] If you are applying to an employer that wants anonymised applications, such as the UK FCDO (research/03 §5.2), your photo is fine on LinkedIn. Just do not put your LinkedIn link inside the anonymised application.

**Banner (the wide image behind your photo)**
- [ ] Simple and calm: a plain colour, a city where you want to work, or a neutral image linked to your field.
- [ ] Optional: one short line of text, such as your positioning in a few words.
- [ ] No employer logo unless your employer allows it.
- [ ] No photos of people you served.
- [ ] Use any simple image tool. LinkedIn shows the recommended size when you upload.

| Setting | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Notify your network of changes** | Turn it off while you edit. Turn it back on after. | So your contacts do not get one alert per edit. |
| **Open to Work** | Choose "Recruiters only" or "All LinkedIn members". You decide. | "All members" adds a visible frame to your photo. Some people find this helps, some find it exposing. |
| **Open to Work if you are employed** | Choose "Recruiters only". Assume your employer could still see it. | LinkedIn tries to hide it from recruiters at your own company but cannot fully promise this. |
| **Job preferences** | List your 2-3 target role titles, locations and contract types. Segment D: add contract and temporary work. | Short-term and consulting work held up better than staff posts (research/01 §7, §8.1 point 6). |
| **Location** | Set the city where you live or have the right to work. | Embassies only hire people who already have the right to work locally (research/03 §5.5). |
| **Public profile** | Make your headline, photo and experience visible to the public. | Recruiters and contacts who are not connected to you can then find you. |
| **Custom profile URL** | Change it to your name, for example linkedin.com/in/[FIRST-NAME]-[LAST-NAME]. | Clean link for your CV and email signature. |
| **Contact info** | Add a professional email. Do not post your phone number publicly. | Fewer scam messages. Fee-charging "recruiters" are scams (research/03 §6.2, §9). |
| **Job alerts** | Set alerts for your target roles. Especially useful for CSR and foundation roles. | LinkedIn is a job source for all segments, especially CSR and foundations (research/03 §10). |
| **Sensitive work** | If your work touches protection cases, advocacy or anything that could put you or others at risk, show less publicly. | Your safety and the safety of the people you served come first. |

### How a round works

| Step | What happens | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Interview | A 20-minute call with Kristin in the week before the drafts are due. She asks what you did, learned and noticed this month. | 20 min |
| 2. Four short drafts | Kristin sends you 4 short drafts: the first 2-3 lines of 4 posts, each in a different format. | Within [POST-DRAFT-TURNAROUND] |
| 3. You choose | You pick the 2 you like best. | 5 min |
| 4. Two full drafts | Kristin writes the 2 full posts, in the language you choose. | Within [POST-DRAFT-TURNAROUND] |
| 5. You publish | You edit if you want, then publish. Space the 2 posts at least a week apart. | 10 min each |

**Questions Kristin may ask in the 20-minute interview**
- What did you work on, study or finish this month?
- What surprised you or changed your mind?
- Who helped you, and how?
- What did you read or hear in the sector that you have an opinion on?
- What is one skill you used that your target employers care about?
- Is there anything you do **not** want to talk about in public?

### Rules for every post

- **Your words, your facts.** Kristin drafts from what you tell her. Nothing goes in that you cannot stand behind in an interview.
- **No confidential information.** No internal figures, no names of people you served, no details of protection or security cases.
- **No photos of the people you served** without their consent and your organisation's permission.
- **No criticism of a current or former employer.** If you lost your job in the cuts, you can say so plainly and without blame.
- **If you are employed, check your employer's social media policy** before posting about your work.
- **Reply to comments** within a day or two. A short, kind reply is enough.
- **Do not post at night when you are low.** If a post is hard to write, it can wait.

### Six post formats for job seekers in this sector

These formats show your skills and thinking without bragging. No format guarantees reach, interviews or a job.

| # | Format | What it is | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What I learned** | One lesson from a project, internship or field visit, and what you would do differently. | All segments, especially A |
| 2 | **My take on a public report** | A short summary of a public report or news item in your field, plus your own view in 2-3 lines. Link the source. | B, C, D |
| 3 | **Mini case** | One real piece of work told as Situation, Action, Result. It doubles as practice for your interview stories (research/03 §3.3). | All segments |
| 4 | **Learning in public** | A course or certificate you finished, and one thing you will use from it. Links to the plan from your gap analysis. | A, C |
| 5 | **Thank you and spotlight** | Thank a mentor, colleague or partner organisation, and say what they taught you or what their work achieves. Network "like a detective": partners and grantees, not only big brands (research/01 §8.1 point 7). | All segments |
| 6 | **What I am looking for** | A clear, calm post about the role you want next, the skills you bring and how people can help. Only when you are comfortable being public. | D, C, and A once the profile is ready |

## Part 3: Outreach scripts

Use these in Week 6 and after. Make every message personal: change at least one line so it is clearly written for that person. Fillers in square brackets are yours to replace.

### Before you send anything

- **Ask for insight, not a job.** You are offering interest and asking for 15 minutes.
- **Not everyone does informational calls.** The Gates Foundation, for example, says it generally does not (research/03 §7). If someone says no or does not reply, it is not personal.
- **Do not contact panel members during a live recruitment.** It can look like you are trying to influence the result. Wait until the process ends.
- **Never pay anyone to get you a job.** Fee-charging "recruiters" are scams. The IRC, for example, never charges fees and never asks for passport numbers in interviews (research/03 §6.2, §9). If someone asks for money or passport details, stop and tell Kristin before you pay or send anything.
- **Write in the language the person works in**, if you can do it well. GIZ, for example, asks candidates to apply in the language of the advert (research/03 §7). The same courtesy works in outreach.

### 1. Connection requests

Keep the note short. LinkedIn limits the length of a note and, on some accounts, how many notes you can send.

**a) Someone whose work you admire (cold)**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], I read your post on [TOPIC] and found it useful. I am a [YOUR-ROLE-OR-STUDIES] interested in [FIELD] in the region. I would be glad to connect and follow your work.

**b) Alumni of your university or programme**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], I am also a [UNIVERSITY] graduate ([YEAR]). I am starting out in [FIELD] and saw you work at [ORGANISATION]. I would love to connect.

**c) After an event or webinar**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], I attended [EVENT] today and your point about [TOPIC] stayed with me. I would be glad to connect.

**d) Referred by someone**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], [MUTUAL-CONTACT] suggested I reach out, as you work on [TOPIC] at [ORGANISATION]. I would be glad to connect.

**e) A recruiter or HR person**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], I am a [YOUR-ROLE] focused on [FIELD] in [REGION]. I follow [ORGANISATION]'s openings and would be glad to connect.

### 2. Informational-call asks

Send this only after they accept your connection, or through a warm introduction.

**a) Standard ask**
> Thank you for connecting, [FIRST-NAME]. I am exploring roles in [FIELD] and your path from [THEIR-PAST-ROLE] to [THEIR-CURRENT-ROLE] is close to what I am aiming for.
>
> Would you have 15 minutes for a short call in the next few weeks? I have two questions: [QUESTION-1] and [QUESTION-2]. I am flexible on timing.

**b) Replying to an expert introduction from Kristin** (Accompaniment: 1 introduction; Accompaniment Plus: 2)

Kristin introduces you in one message and has already shared your three questions with the expert. The expert offers 2-3 times. You book one and send the link. The call is 30 minutes, and you never ask for a job, a referral or to pass on your CV.
> Hello [FIRST-NAME], thank you for agreeing to talk, and thank you Kristin for the introduction. [CHOSEN-TIME] works well for me. I will send the meeting link today. I look forward to hearing your view on my three questions.

**c) Someone more senior (moving up, segment B)**
> Hello [FIRST-NAME], I have followed your work at [ORGANISATION] on [TOPIC]. I am working towards a [TARGET-ROLE] role and would value 15 minutes of your advice on how you made that step. I know your time is limited, so I will keep it short.

**Questions that work well in a 15-minute call**
- What does a strong candidate for [ROLE] look like in your team?
- Which skills are hardest to find right now?
- Where do roles like this get advertised in the region?
- Is there anyone else you think I should talk to?

### 3. Follow-ups

Send **one** follow-up only, about 7-10 days after your first message.

**a) No reply**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], just following up on my message from [DATE]. I know your schedule is busy. If now is not a good time, no problem at all.

**b) After a call, sharing progress (2-4 weeks later)**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], a quick update: I followed your advice on [TOPIC] and [ACTION-YOU-TOOK]. Thank you again for your time.

**c) After you applied to their organisation**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], I wanted to let you know I applied for [ROLE] at [ORGANISATION], following our conversation. Thank you again for your insight on the team.

Do not ask a contact to influence a selection. Telling them you applied is enough.

### 4. Thank-yous

**a) After an informational call (same day)**
> Thank you for your time today, [FIRST-NAME]. Your advice on [TOPIC] was very useful, and I will start with [NEXT-STEP]. I will keep you posted.

After an expert call that Kristin arranged, use the expert thank-you template Kristin gives you, within 24 hours.

**b) After a referral or introduction**
> Thank you for introducing me to [NAME], [FIRST-NAME]. We spoke on [DATE] and it was very helpful. I appreciate you thinking of me.

**c) At the end of your programme (Week 6 homework)**
> Hi [FIRST-NAME], over the past few weeks you helped me with [WHAT-THEY-DID]. Thank you. Here is where I am now: [ONE-LINE-UPDATE]. I hope I can return the favour one day.

### French notes

- Always use **"vous"** in a first message.
- Peers on LinkedIn often write "Bonjour [Prénom]". For someone senior, or if unsure, use "Bonjour Madame [Nom]" or "Bonjour Monsieur [Nom]".
- Close with **"Bien cordialement"** and your name.
- Choose one gender form for yourself (diplômé or diplômée). Do not write "diplômé(e)" in a personal message.

| Message | French version |
|---|---|
| Connection request | Bonjour [Prénom], je suis [Nom], jeune diplômée en [domaine]. Votre travail chez [Organisation] sur [sujet] m'intéresse beaucoup. Je serais ravie de rejoindre votre réseau. Bien cordialement, [Nom] |
| Informational-call ask | Merci d'avoir accepté ma demande. Accepteriez-vous un échange de 15 minutes dans les prochaines semaines ? J'aimerais vous poser deux questions sur [sujet]. Je m'adapte à vos disponibilités. |
| Follow-up | Je me permets de revenir vers vous au sujet de mon message du [date]. Si ce n'est pas le bon moment, aucun souci. |
| Thank-you | Merci beaucoup pour votre temps aujourd'hui. Votre conseil sur [sujet] m'a beaucoup aidée. Je vais commencer par [action] et je vous tiendrai au courant. |

The examples above use the feminine form. Change to "diplômé", "ravi" and "aidé" if that is your form.

### Arabic notes

- For a first message, use **Modern Standard Arabic**. Switch to your shared dialect only if you already know the person or they write to you in dialect.
- Match the gender of the person you write to: تسمح for a man, تسمحين for a woman.
- Use titles where they fit: أستاذ / أستاذة, دكتور / دكتورة.
- Keep it short. A long, formal opening reads as a form letter on LinkedIn.
- If you mix in English names of organisations, read the message once before sending. Right-to-left and left-to-right text can jump around.
- Close with **مع خالص التحية** and your name.

| Message | Arabic version |
|---|---|
| Connection request | مرحباً [الاسم]، أنا [اسمك]، تخرّجت حديثاً في [المجال] من [الجامعة]. اطّلعت على عملك في [المنظمة] حول [الموضوع] وأودّ أن أتعلّم من تجربتك. يسعدني التواصل معك. |
| Informational-call ask | شكراً لقبول طلب التواصل. هل تسمح لي بمكالمة قصيرة مدتها ١٥ دقيقة خلال الأسابيع القادمة؟ لديّ سؤالان عن [الموضوع]، وأنا مرن في التوقيت بما يناسبك. |
| Follow-up | أعود إليك بخصوص رسالتي السابقة. أعرف أن جدولك مزدحم، وإن لم يكن الوقت مناسباً الآن فلا مشكلة إطلاقاً. |

The examples above are written to a man and from a man where the grammar needs it. For a woman reader, use تسمحين. If you are a woman, write مرنة instead of مرن. Ask Kristin to check your first few Arabic messages.

## Part 4: Your weekly 30-minute routine

Run this once a week, alone, at a fixed time. Put it in your calendar, like your application time from Week 4. Set a timer. Close LinkedIn when it rings.

| Minutes | Do this |
|---|---|
| **0-5** | **Inbox.** Reply to messages. Accept connection requests that fit your targets. Ignore or report anyone who asks for money. |
| **5-15** | **Reach out.** Send 3-5 connection requests to people on your contact map (Week 4), each with a personal note. Send 1 follow-up if one is due. |
| **15-25** | **Engage.** Leave 3 thoughtful comments on posts in your target field. Add something: a question, an example, a short view. "Great post" does not count. |
| **25-30** | **Record.** Add any role you found yourself on LinkedIn to your job tracker, so Kristin can check it. Update your contact map: who replied, who to follow up with next week, any call booked. |

**Once a month (add 10 minutes)**
- Re-read your headline and About section. Do they still match the roles you are going for?
- Add any new certificate, course or result.
- Accompaniment Plus: check which post drafts are waiting to be published.

**In your weekly written check-in**, under "What did you send, do or finish this week?", add one line: requests sent, replies received, calls booked.

**After Week 6**, this routine becomes part of your 90-day plan. It is the same 30 minutes, without anyone checking.

**A note on how LinkedIn can feel.** The feed is full of "I'm happy to announce" posts. On a hard week, that can sting, especially after a layoff or a long silence. Searches in this sector can take months, and silence after applying is normal. Some employers never reply to candidates they do not select (research/03 §6.1; research/01 §8.1 point 9). If the routine starts to drain you, do only the first 15 minutes that week and tell Kristin in your check-in.
